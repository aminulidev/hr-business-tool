Overtime compensation is one of the most frequent sources of confusion, payroll errors, and expensive legal risks for small and medium-sized businesses. Wage and hour lawsuits initiated by disgruntled employees (and heavily backed by the Department of Labor) can cost companies millions in back pay and damages.

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), most non-exempt employees must be paid overtime for any hours worked over 40 in a single, fixed workweek. However, the details of how to calculate this, who qualifies, and what bonuses are included can get complicated fast.

## The Federal Time-and-a-Half Rule

The foundational rule of the FLSA is the "time-and-a-half" requirement. The standard overtime rate must be at least 1.5 times the employee's "regular rate of pay." 

For example, if an employee's standard hourly rate is $20 per hour, their overtime rate must be at least $30 per hour for every hour worked beyond the 40-hour threshold in that week. Note that the FLSA applies per workweek; you cannot average hours over a two-week pay period to avoid paying overtime (e.g., an employee working 50 hours week one and 30 hours week two is still owed 10 hours of overtime).

## The Big Trap: Exempt vs. Non-Exempt Employees

Not all employees are legally entitled to overtime. "Exempt" employees are exactly that—exempt from the FLSA's overtime requirements. These are typically salaried workers in executive, administrative, professional, or outside sales roles.

However, a common misconception is that paying someone a salary automatically makes them exempt. This is false. To be truly exempt, an employee must meet three strict criteria:
1.  **Salary Basis:** They are paid a predetermined and fixed salary that is not subject to reduction because of variations in the quality or quantity of work.
2.  **Salary Level:** They earn above the federally mandated minimum salary threshold (which updates periodically).
3.  **Duties Test:** Their actual day-to-day work—not just their job title—must involve high-level decision-making, management of others, or advanced specialized knowledge. 

If they fail the duties test, they are non-exempt and must receive overtime, regardless of their high salary.

## Calculating Overtime for Complex Pay Structures

Calculating overtime is simple when an employee makes a flat hourly wage. It becomes incredibly complex when you introduce performance bonuses, commissions, or multiple pay rates.

Under the FLSA, all non-discretionary bonuses (bonuses that are promised based on meeting specific metrics) must be retroactively included in the employee's "regular rate of pay" before calculating the overtime rate. 

Furthermore, if an employee works two different jobs at your company at two different hourly rates, you must calculate their overtime using a "weighted average" of the two rates.

## State-Specific Overtime Laws

The FLSA sets the federal minimum standard, but states are free to enforce stricter rules that favor the employee. As an employer, you must comply with whichever law (state or federal) provides the greater benefit to the worker.

*   **Daily Overtime:** States like California, Alaska, and Nevada have daily overtime laws. For instance, in California, an employee must be paid time-and-a-half for any hours worked beyond eight in a single day, regardless of whether they hit 40 hours for the week. 
*   **Double Time:** Some states require "double time" (2x the regular rate) for hours worked beyond 12 in a single day or for hours worked on the seventh consecutive day of a workweek.
