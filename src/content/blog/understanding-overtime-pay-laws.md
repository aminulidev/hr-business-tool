Overtime compensation is one of the most frequent sources of confusion, payroll errors, and expensive legal risks for small and medium-sized businesses. Wage and hour lawsuits — initiated by disgruntled employees and heavily backed by the Department of Labor — can cost companies millions in back pay, damages, and legal fees.

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), most non-exempt employees must be paid overtime for any hours worked over 40 in a single, fixed workweek. However, the details of how to calculate this, who qualifies, what bonuses must be included, and how state laws differ can get complicated fast.

This guide breaks down everything you need to know to stay compliant.

## The Foundation: Federal Time-and-a-Half Rule

The foundational rule of the FLSA is the **time-and-a-half requirement**. The standard overtime rate must be at least **1.5 times the employee's "regular rate of pay"** for every hour worked beyond 40 in a single workweek.

**The basic calculation:**

> **Overtime Pay = Overtime Hours × (Regular Rate × 1.5)**

**Example:** An employee earns $22/hour and works 47 hours in a week.
- Regular pay: 40 hours × $22 = $880
- Overtime hours: 7 hours
- Overtime rate: $22 × 1.5 = $33/hour
- Overtime pay: 7 × $33 = $231
- **Total weekly pay: $1,111**

**Critical rule on workweek averaging:** The FLSA applies overtime on a **per workweek basis**. You cannot average hours across a two-week pay period to avoid overtime. An employee who works 50 hours in week one and 30 hours in week two is still owed 10 hours of overtime for week one, even if the two-week average is exactly 40 hours.

## Who Is Entitled to Overtime? (Exempt vs. Non-Exempt)

Not all employees are legally entitled to overtime. "Exempt" employees are exactly that — exempt from the FLSA's overtime requirements. These are typically salaried workers in executive, administrative, professional, or outside sales roles.

A common and costly misconception is that paying someone a salary automatically makes them exempt. **This is false.** To be truly exempt from overtime, an employee must meet **all three** of these strict criteria:

### 1. Salary Basis Test
The employee must be paid a predetermined and fixed salary that is not subject to reduction because of variations in the quality or quantity of work performed in any particular week.

### 2. Salary Level Test
The employee must earn at or above the federally mandated minimum salary threshold. As of July 1, 2024:
- **Standard exemptions:** $684/week ($35,568/year)
- **Highly Compensated Employees (HCE):** $107,432/year

Many states set significantly higher thresholds — California requires $66,560/year (2024) for most exemptions.

### 3. Duties Test
The employee's **actual day-to-day job duties** — not their job title — must qualify for one of the recognized exemption categories: executive, administrative, professional, computer, or outside sales.

**The trap:** A "salaried" receptionist, warehouse lead, or customer service team lead who earns $45,000/year may pass the salary tests but fail the duties test. They exercise little independent judgment on significant matters. Result: they are **salaried non-exempt** and must receive overtime for every hour over 40 — retroactively.

## Calculating Overtime for Complex Pay Structures

The overtime calculation becomes significantly more complicated when employees have variable pay structures. The FLSA requires that overtime be calculated on the employee's **"regular rate of pay"** — not just their base hourly rate.

### Including Non-Discretionary Bonuses

Under the FLSA, **all non-discretionary bonuses** must be retroactively folded into the regular rate before calculating overtime. A non-discretionary bonus is any bonus that is promised based on meeting specific performance metrics — production bonuses, attendance bonuses, quality bonuses, and sales commissions all fall into this category.

**Worked example:**
An employee earns $18/hour and works 48 hours in a week. They also earned a $160 production bonus that week.

Step 1 — Calculate total straight-time earnings:
- Hourly wages: 48 hours × $18 = $864
- Bonus: $160
- Total: $1,024

Step 2 — Calculate the regular rate:
- Regular rate = $1,024 ÷ 48 hours = $21.33/hour

Step 3 — Calculate the overtime premium (the "half-time" owed in addition to straight-time wages already paid):
- Overtime premium: 8 hours × ($21.33 × 0.5) = $85.32

Step 4 — Total compensation:
- $1,024 (straight-time including bonus) + $85.32 (OT premium) = **$1,109.32**

This is meaningfully different from the naive calculation of $864 + 8×($18×1.5) = $864 + $216 = $1,080. The correct amount is **$29.32 higher**, and failing to calculate it correctly on every paycheck compounds into a significant liability over time.

### Multiple Pay Rates (Weighted Average Method)

If an employee works two different jobs at the same employer during the same workweek at different rates, the overtime rate is calculated using the **weighted average** of the two rates.

**Example:** An employee works 30 hours as a supervisor at $25/hour and 16 hours as a floor worker at $18/hour (46 total hours):
- Total straight-time earnings: (30 × $25) + (16 × $18) = $750 + $288 = $1,038
- Regular rate = $1,038 ÷ 46 hours = $22.57/hour
- Overtime hours: 6
- Overtime premium: 6 × ($22.57 × 0.5) = $67.71
- Total pay: $1,038 + $67.71 = **$1,105.71**

## State-by-State Overtime Law Comparison

The FLSA sets the federal minimum standard, but states are free to enforce stricter rules. As an employer, you must always comply with whichever law — state or federal — provides the **greater benefit to the worker**.

### States With Daily Overtime Rules

**California:** California has the most worker-protective overtime laws in the country:
- Over 8 hours in a single day → 1.5× regular rate
- Over 12 hours in a single day → 2× regular rate (double-time)
- 7th consecutive day worked in a workweek → 1.5× for first 8 hours
- Over 8 hours on the 7th consecutive day → 2× regular rate

**Alaska:** Daily overtime kicks in after 8 hours in a workday, even if the weekly total is below 40 hours.

**Nevada:** Daily overtime required for hours worked over 8 in a day for employees earning less than 1.5× Nevada's minimum wage.

**Colorado:** Daily overtime after 12 hours worked in a day or 12 consecutive hours regardless of start/end time, plus weekly overtime after 40 hours.

### States That Follow Federal FLSA Only

The majority of states, including Texas, Florida, Illinois, Georgia, Ohio, and Pennsylvania, follow the federal FLSA standard: overtime is required only for hours above 40 per workweek. There is no daily overtime trigger in these states.

### States With Higher Minimum Thresholds for Exemption

| State | Minimum Weekly Salary for Exemption |
|---|---|
| California | $1,280/week ($66,560/year) as of 2024 |
| New York (NYC) | $1,237.50/week as of 2024 |
| Washington | $1,302.40/week as of 2024 |
| Colorado | $1,057.69/week as of 2024 |
| Federal (FLSA) | $684/week ($35,568/year) |

An employee in California earning $40,000/year may be exempt under federal law but is **not exempt under California law** and must receive state overtime.

## Common Overtime Compliance Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Off-the-Clock Work

If an employer knows (or should know) that an employee is working, that time must be counted and compensated — even if the employee wasn't authorized to work overtime. Common scenarios:
- Checking and responding to work emails after hours
- Working through a mandatory meal break
- Arriving early to set up before their official shift begins
- Attending mandatory training outside of scheduled hours

### Mistake 2: Misusing the "Comp Time" Arrangement

In the private sector, **comp time (compensatory time off instead of overtime pay) is illegal under the FLSA.** You cannot tell a non-exempt employee "you worked 5 hours of overtime this week, so take 5 hours off next week instead of paying you extra." Only state and federal government employers can legally use comp time for non-exempt employees.

### Mistake 3: Averaging Hours Across Pay Periods

As noted earlier, overtime is calculated per workweek, not per pay period. If you run biweekly payroll (every two weeks), each of the two weeks within that period must be independently analyzed for overtime. You cannot average the 80 hours across the pay period.

### Mistake 4: Not Including All Required Compensation in the Regular Rate

The regular rate must include: base hourly wages, shift differentials, non-discretionary bonuses, commissions, piece-rate pay, and the fair value of certain non-cash items. What does NOT need to be included: discretionary bonuses (true surprise bonuses), gifts, vacation pay, holiday pay, and overtime premiums themselves.

## Employer Compliance Checklist

Use this checklist to audit your current overtime compliance posture:

- [ ] Audit every salaried employee against all three FLSA exemption tests (salary basis, salary level, duties test)
- [ ] Verify salary levels meet your state's (potentially higher) thresholds
- [ ] Ensure all non-discretionary bonuses and commissions are folded into the regular rate when calculating overtime
- [ ] Confirm your timekeeping system captures all time worked, including pre-shift setup and post-shift closedown
- [ ] Review your meal break policy — are employees truly relieved of all duties, or are they working through breaks?
- [ ] Verify that workweek definitions (e.g., Monday–Sunday) are established, documented, and consistent
- [ ] Confirm comp time is not being used as an overtime substitute for non-exempt employees
- [ ] Ensure multi-state employers are applying state-specific daily overtime rules in applicable states
- [ ] Review any piece-rate or commission employees for correct regular rate calculation methodology
- [ ] Document all exempt classification decisions and the factual basis for each one

## Key Takeaways

- Federal overtime is required at 1.5× the regular rate for all hours over 40 in a workweek
- Regular rate includes all non-discretionary bonuses, commissions, and shift differentials — not just base hourly pay
- Salaried employees are NOT automatically exempt — all three FLSA tests (salary basis, level, and duties) must be met
- California, Alaska, Nevada, and Colorado have stricter daily overtime triggers that apply regardless of weekly total
- Several states set significantly higher minimum salary thresholds for exemption than the federal floor
- Comp time is illegal in the private sector as a substitute for overtime pay
- Overtime is calculated per workweek — you cannot average hours across pay periods

Maintaining compliance with overtime laws is not just about avoiding devastating lawsuits; it's about building trust and maintaining morale with your workforce. Regularly audit your employee classifications, ensure your payroll software correctly handles your state requirements, and when in doubt, consult an employment attorney before the Department of Labor finds the issue first.
