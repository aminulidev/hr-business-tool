When scaling a business and defining a new role, one of the first and most critical decisions you'll face is whether to offer a fixed salary or an hourly wage. This isn't just a matter of preference; your choice impacts your payroll budget, employee morale, administrative overhead, and strict legal compliance under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA).

Both models have distinct advantages and drawbacks. Choosing the right one requires a deep understanding of the role itself, industry norms, and your long-term business goals. This guide covers everything you need to know to make the right call.

## Understanding the Core Difference

At its most fundamental level:

- **Hourly (Non-Exempt):** The employee is paid for each hour worked. If they work more than 40 hours in a workweek, they are legally entitled to overtime pay (typically 1.5× their regular rate under FLSA).
- **Salaried (Exempt):** The employee receives a fixed, predetermined amount per pay period regardless of the exact number of hours they work in any given week.

The critical word in the salaried definition is "exempt" — exempt from overtime requirements. However, **not all salaried employees are automatically exempt**, and this distinction is where most employer mistakes happen.

## The Case for Hourly Pay (Non-Exempt)

Hourly employees are paid strictly for the exact amount of time they are working. This model is standard in retail, hospitality, manufacturing, customer service, warehousing, and many part-time or entry-level professional roles.

### Advantages of Hourly Pay

**1. Fairness and transparency.** Employees feel compensated for every minute they give to the company. There is no ambiguity about pay — hours worked × rate = pay. This simplicity builds trust, especially with frontline workers who are skeptical of "salaried" arrangements that might mean unlimited unpaid hours.

**2. Direct cost control.** You only pay for the labor you actually need. During slow seasons, you can reduce hours to immediately trim payroll costs. During peak seasons, you can scale up hours and even authorize overtime selectively, giving you granular control over your single largest business expense.

**3. Legal clarity on overtime.** Hourly workers are almost universally "non-exempt," meaning they are legally entitled to time-and-a-half pay for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek under federal law. This clarity removes the exemption ambiguity that trips up so many employers.

**4. Compliance with minimum wage laws.** Because hourly workers are paid by the hour, compliance with federal and state minimum wage laws is mechanically enforced — if you pay at or above the minimum hourly rate, you're in compliance. There's no question of whether the effective hourly rate from a salary meets the minimum wage for a week where the employee worked 50 hours.

### Disadvantages of Hourly Pay

**1. Administrative burden.** Tracking hours, managing timesheets, policing break requirements, reviewing clock-in/clock-out records, and calculating variable paychecks requires significant administrative oversight and reliable time-tracking software. For businesses with 20+ hourly workers, this overhead is substantial.

**2. Variable payroll costs.** Your payroll expenses fluctuate week to week based on hours worked, making tight financial forecasting harder. A particularly busy week with approved overtime can blow a monthly payroll budget by 15–20%.

**3. Cultural perception.** In some professional environments, an hourly arrangement is perceived negatively — it can signal a lack of trust or an entry-level classification. Knowledge workers and professionals often prefer the status and autonomy associated with salaried roles.

## The Case for Salaried Pay (Exempt)

Salaried employees receive a guaranteed, fixed amount of pay per pay period, regardless of whether they work 35 hours or 55 hours in a given week. This is standard for professional, managerial, technical, and executive roles.

### Advantages of Salaried Pay

**1. Budget predictability.** Your payroll costs are stable and highly predictable, making financial planning, cash flow forecasting, and budget modeling dramatically easier. You can build a P&L with confidence knowing exactly what your HR line item will be every month.

**2. Reduced administrative overhead.** You don't need to meticulously track minutes, manage timesheets, monitor break compliance, or calculate fluctuating overtime rates. Salaried roles dramatically simplify your payroll process and reduce the HR administrative burden.

**3. Focus on outcomes, not hours.** Salaried roles naturally shift the organizational focus from "time in seat" toward actual output, results, and project completion. This philosophical alignment is critical for creative, strategic, and leadership work where the value delivered cannot be measured in hours.

**4. Easier benefits administration.** Most benefits eligibility (health insurance, retirement plans, PTO accrual) is structured around full-time salaried status. Administering benefits for consistent headcount is significantly simpler than managing benefits for a variable-hour workforce.

### Disadvantages of Salaried Pay

**1. Risk of overwork and burnout.** Because there is no financial penalty for the employer when a salaried employee works 60 hours a week, there is a natural temptation — consciously or not — to overload these employees. Long-term excessive work hours without additional compensation is a leading driver of burnout and turnover among salaried professionals.

**2. Fixed cost regardless of output.** If business slows dramatically, your salaried payroll costs remain constant. Unlike hourly workers whose hours can be reduced, salaried employees must generally be fully paid or formally laid off — there is no middle ground without legal risk.

**3. Misclassification exposure.** This is the biggest legal trap in HR. You cannot simply put someone on a salary to avoid paying them overtime. To be legally exempt from overtime, the employee must meet strict FLSA tests (see below). Getting this wrong exposes the business to back pay liability, penalties, and lawsuits.

## The Legal Trap: Exempt vs. Non-Exempt Status

The most consequential and most misunderstood aspect of the salary vs. hourly decision is **exemption status**.

Under the FLSA, an employee must meet **all three** of the following criteria to be legally exempt from overtime:

### Test 1: Salary Basis Test
The employee must be paid a predetermined, fixed salary that is not subject to reduction based on variations in the quality or quantity of work performed in any given week. If you dock a salaried employee's pay because they missed a few hours on a Tuesday, you've likely destroyed their exempt status for that week.

**Permitted deductions from a salaried employee's pay:**
- Full-week absences for personal reasons
- Full-week absences due to sickness (if a bona fide sick leave policy applies)
- Penalties for violating major safety rules
- First or last weeks of employment (partial weeks)

**Prohibited deductions that destroy exempt status:**
- Partial-day absences (except under a compliant PTO policy)
- Budget-related pay cuts during slow periods
- Deductions for equipment damage

### Test 2: Salary Level Test
The employee must earn above the federally mandated minimum salary threshold. As of July 1, 2024 (the most recent update), the federal minimum salary for exempt employees is:

- **Standard exemption:** $684/week ($35,568/year)
- **Highly Compensated Employees (HCE):** $107,432/year

**Important:** Many states have set their own, higher salary level thresholds. California requires $66,560/year (2024) for professional exemptions — nearly double the federal floor.

### Test 3: Duties Test
This is the most nuanced and frequently misapplied test. The employee's **primary job duties** — not their title — must involve exempt-level work, which falls into one of these categories:

**Executive Exemption:** Primary duty is management of the enterprise or a department; regularly directs work of two or more full-time employees; has authority to hire/fire or significant weight in such decisions.

**Administrative Exemption:** Primary duty involves office or non-manual work directly related to management or general business operations; primary duty includes the exercise of discretion and independent judgment with respect to matters of significance.

**Professional Exemption:** Primary duty involves work requiring advanced knowledge in a field of science or learning customarily acquired through a prolonged course of specialized intellectual instruction; or primary duty involves invention, imagination, originality, or talent in a recognized field of artistic or creative endeavor.

**The key trap:** A salaried receptionist who earns $45,000/year meets the salary basis and salary level tests but almost certainly does NOT meet the duties test — they do not exercise the required level of discretion and independent judgment on matters of significance. This person is **salaried non-exempt** and must receive overtime for hours worked beyond 40 per week.

## Tax Implications: Hourly vs. Salary

From a payroll tax perspective, the mechanics are largely the same — both hourly and salaried wages are subject to federal income tax withholding, FICA (Social Security and Medicare), and state income tax. However, there are practical differences:

**For hourly workers:** Variable paychecks mean variable withholding. An employee who works a lot of overtime in a pay period will have a larger paycheck, which may push them into a higher withholding bracket for that period, even if their annual income doesn't justify it. This can lead to overwithholding and a larger tax refund — or surprises if overtime bonuses push them into a higher effective rate.

**For salaried workers:** Fixed paychecks make tax planning straightforward. Withholding is highly consistent period to period, making it easier for employees to accurately predict their tax liability.

## Hybrid Models: Salaried Non-Exempt

One option that is often overlooked is the **salaried non-exempt** arrangement — a fixed weekly salary that does NOT meet the FLSA duties test, so overtime is still owed, but the pay is structured as a salary for ease of administration.

In this model, the employer sets a "regular rate of pay" by dividing the employee's weekly salary by their expected regular hours. Overtime is then calculated on that regular rate.

**Example:** An employee is paid $800/week and expected to work 40 hours.
- Regular rate = $800 ÷ 40 = $20/hour
- If they work 45 hours in a week:
  - Regular pay: $800 (salary covers 40 hours)
  - Overtime premium: 5 hours × ($20 × 0.5) = $50 (the "half-time" premium for the OT hours)
  - Total pay: $850

This is sometimes called the "fluctuating workweek" method, and it can legally result in a lower effective overtime rate than standard time-and-a-half, provided specific conditions are met. Consult an employment attorney before implementing this model.

## Making the Decision: A Practical Framework

**Choose hourly when:**
- The role involves task-based, measurable work that varies week to week
- The business has significant seasonal or demand-driven workload fluctuations
- You are hiring part-time or entry-level workers
- Time-tracking infrastructure is already in place
- The role does not clearly meet the FLSA duties test for exemption

**Choose salaried when:**
- The role involves strategic, creative, or management work that cannot be measured in hours
- The employee has genuine autonomy and discretion in their work
- Budget predictability is a priority
- The role clearly meets all three FLSA exemption tests
- You want to attract senior professional talent who expect salaried compensation

## Key Takeaways

- Hourly is better for roles with variable workloads, entry-level positions, and jobs requiring strict hours tracking
- Salaried is better for professional, managerial, and creative roles where output matters more than hours
- "Salaried" does NOT automatically mean "exempt from overtime" — the FLSA duties test must be met
- Many small businesses unknowingly misclassify salaried non-exempt workers, creating significant back-pay liability
- Both models carry the same payroll tax obligations — the key difference is the fixed vs. variable nature of the pay

There is no one-size-fits-all answer. Successful, scalable businesses typically employ a strategic mix of both models, tailored to specific roles and departments. Understanding the legal limitations and financial implications of your compensation structure is the first step toward building a compliant, motivated, and financially sustainable workforce.
