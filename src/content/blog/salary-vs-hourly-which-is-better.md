When scaling a business and defining a new role, one of the first and most critical decisions you'll face is whether to offer a fixed salary or an hourly wage. This isn't just a matter of preference; your choice impacts your payroll budget, employee morale, administrative overhead, and strict legal compliance under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA).

Both models have distinct advantages and drawbacks. Choosing the right one requires a deep understanding of the role itself, the industry norms, and your long-term business goals.

## The Case for Hourly Pay (Non-Exempt)

Hourly employees are paid strictly for the exact amount of time they are working. This model is the standard in retail, hospitality, manufacturing, customer service, and many part-time or entry-level roles.

### The Pros of Hourly Pay
*   **Ultimate Fairness:** Employees feel compensated for every minute they give to the company. If they work longer, they earn more.
*   **Cost Control and Flexibility:** You only pay for the labor you actually need. During slow seasons, you can reduce hours to save on payroll costs.
*   **Clear Overtime Rules:** Hourly workers are almost universally "non-exempt," meaning they are legally entitled to time-and-a-half pay for any hours worked over 40 in a workweek. This clarity prevents legal ambiguity.

### The Cons of Hourly Pay
*   **Administrative Burden:** Tracking hours, managing timesheets, policing breaks, and calculating variable paychecks requires significant administrative oversight and reliable software.
*   **Variable Costs:** Your payroll expenses fluctuate week to week, making tight financial forecasting more difficult.
*   **Cultural Divide:** In some organizations, hourly workers may feel like "second-class citizens" compared to their salaried counterparts who enjoy more flexibility.

## The Case for Salaried Pay (Exempt)

Salaried employees receive a guaranteed, fixed amount of pay per pay period, regardless of whether they work 35 hours or 50 hours in a given week. This is standard for professional, managerial, and executive roles.

### The Pros of Salaried Pay
*   **Budget Predictability:** Your payroll costs are stable and highly predictable, making financial planning much easier.
*   **Reduced Administration:** You don't need to meticulously track minutes, manage complex timesheets, or calculate fluctuating overtime rates. 
*   **Focus on Results:** Salaried roles naturally shift the focus away from "time in seat" toward actual output, results, and project completion. This often aligns better with creative, strategic, or management work.

### The Cons of Salaried Pay
*   **Risk of Burnout:** Because there is no financial penalty for the employer when a salaried employee works 60 hours a week, there is a temptation to overwork them. This leads to severe burnout and high turnover.
*   **Misclassification Risks:** This is a massive legal trap. You cannot simply put someone on a salary to avoid paying them overtime. To be "exempt" from overtime, the employee must pass strict FLSA tests regarding their salary level and job duties.

## The Legal Trap: Exemption Status

The biggest mistake employers make is assuming "Salary = No Overtime." Under the FLSA, an employee must meet three criteria to be exempt from overtime:
1.  **Salary Basis Test:** They must be paid a guaranteed minimum amount each pay period.
2.  **Salary Level Test:** They must earn above a specific federal threshold (which has seen significant increases in recent years).
3.  **Duties Test:** Their primary job duties must involve executive, administrative, or professional level work that requires independent judgment and discretion.

If an employee is paid a salary but does not meet the Duties Test (e.g., a salaried receptionist), they are "Salaried Non-Exempt" and must still be paid overtime.

There is no one-size-fits-all answer. Successful, scalable businesses usually employ a strategic mix of both models, heavily tailored to specific roles and departments. Understanding the legal limitations and the cultural impact of your compensation structure is the first step toward building a sustainable, motivated workforce.
